Checking or Recomputing? Two Cost Regimes in Verifying AI-Generated Formal Artifacts
Yusheng Zheng UC Santa Cruz yzhen165@ucsc.edu

Abstract
Checking a stored proof can avoid its original search, while checking a computational certiﬁcate can repeat the calculation that justiﬁes it. We measure a pinned AI-generated Lean library and build ProofServe, an original-kernel check/audit tool. Matched target windows retain 80.28–82.35% of source-compilation wall time for two computational certiﬁcates, versus 0.46–6.54% for two ordinary controls. Across 31 module-process pairs, the median checker/compiler CPU ratio is 0.451, with overlapping source-proxy classes. 162,170 of 163,096 modules (99.43%) completed, using 168.956 P / 302.399 E CPU-hours; 926 were deferred. These are separate observed-work lower bounds; the missing tail is not imputed. The full-frame import graph omits 19.78% of source ﬁles and 33.30% of lexical kernel-decision sites. ProofServe integrates dependency-aware parallel replay, restartable segments and scoped receipts. From one to eight workers, Nat.Basic/Grid/Barrier wall speedups are 1.70/2.14/1.73×, with higher CPU. By sharing kernel-checked theorem bodies, default mode can accept crafted artifacts that ofﬁcial checking rejects in their original import context. Strict isolation costs 4.23× wall on Nat.Basic at eight workers.
1 Introduction
Proof-embedded computation can preserve expensive work across the boundary between producing and checking an AI-generated proof. Lean validates elaborated terms rather than repeating tactic search, but deﬁnitional equality can still evaluate a program inside a term. Users therefore need both a cost model and a precise account of what a successful project check covers.
Existing work already establishes the value of independent checking. Lean 4.35.0 adds lake check to export and recheck default targets, following the FRO’s external-checking agenda [8, 6]. A public report on the same OpenAI library performs a complete cloud build and extensive independent checking [10]. It proposes kernel computation as an explanation for expensive checks from three source examples. Our empirical increment is a matched module-level measurement of that explanation, an observed dependency preﬁx with explicit

condition-dependent lower bounds, and a coverage analysis at a different corpus revision. Our systems contribution is ProofServe’s original-kernel workﬂow and auditable receipts, evaluated through its release-form interface. The four questions follow these boundaries:
RQ1 How do checking costs differ between stored mathematical proofs and computational certiﬁcates?
RQ2 What work is observed in the measured dependency preﬁx?
RQ3 How does parallel original-kernel checking trade wall time, CPU work and additional trusted code?
RQ4 What do default builds, repeated verdicts and axiom receipts establish about veriﬁcation coverage?
2 Method and workload
A per-module observed ledger and matched checking windows separate measured library work from selected-case checking behavior. Our primary corpus is the published OpenAI mathematics repository [11], pinned at 4eaee875: 163,096 executable modules plus one conﬁguration unit, Lean 4.34.1 and Mathlib d13f23b7. The original 42 compilation observations and their strata remain historical calibration; they never ﬁll the unmeasured tail.
Source cost includes a module’s imports, tactics, kernel checking and artifact emission. Separate prerequisite preparation, C compilation and build-driver work are outside this clock. CPU means user plus system time. Whole-process replay and incremental target windows are distinct measurements; Figure 1 labels both. Reported sample and release timings use CPUs 0–7 of an i9-14900K, one task at a time, retaining ﬁle caches. Lean runs omit -M, including -M0; external memory protection is recorded separately.
The public package data/openai-math-4eaee875/ records sample identities, raw-cost provenance, clocks and estimates. This paper pins its original-sample summary to 1cc14067. Historical replay measurements explain cost boundaries; system performance must come from committed release-tool results in checker/results/ and data/external-projects/.

3.1 Computational certiﬁcates retain work
Matched target windows show expensive replay for computational certiﬁcates and cheap replay for ordinary controls. In Figure 1(b), Grid and Amplitudes retain 80.28% and 82.35% of their source compiler’s wall time. Their replay components are 255.489 and 110.026 seconds; almost all of each is inside the kernel replay call. Mathlib Nat.Basic and NearPin retain 6.54% and 0.46%. Each target component reads, maps and checks that target after dependency preparation; each denominator is that module’s compiler process. These shared boundaries quantify the expensive kernel replay anticipated by the report’s computation hypothesis in the selected cases [10].
The mechanism is visible in the proof boundary. A tactic can search for a mathematical derivation and emit a term that records its successful steps. Replaying that term validates those steps without reconstructing the search. A ﬁnite certiﬁcate justiﬁed through of_decide_eq_true instead asks the kernel to validate the decision procedure’s result through reduction. The compact wrapper can therefore retain evaluation over a large ﬁnite object. Our target measurement holds the declared input and original checking operation ﬁxed and locates that retained cost in the replay window. Its practical consequence is that source length and a cheap closure average are weak planning signals for an individual certiﬁcate.
The broader paired panel shows variation rather than a clean binary split. Across 31 original modules, checker/compiler CPU has median 0.451: 0.391 for the 14 unmarked and 0.498 for the 17 marked modules. An explicit comment/string-masked decide +kernel site is a reproducible proxy for computation, while ordinary decide can also encode substantial evaluation [2, 5]. 4 ProofServe design
ProofServe turns the cost and coverage ﬁndings into separate check, audit and compatibility-only serve interfaces. Its original-kernel route preserves mathematical checking rules while adding explicit coordination and artifact trust.
4.1 Check and resume a dependency closure
A checked preﬁx amortizes preparation while preserving each module’s declared dependency context. proofserve check <project> discovers compiled local roots or uses explicit roots, traverses imports, and reports missing artifacts. The checker builds a graph from ordinary declarations’ referenced constants. Ready declarations run against a checked frontier using the original kernel operation; the coordinator validates returned identities and commits the next frontier after all workers in a wave join. Nonordinary declarations and their ordinary prerequisites use the original replay path before the remaining graph runs. Unsupported local structure retains conservative fallback.
Review-driven repairs and theorem-body policy. The ﬁrst read-only code review found input-ﬁle shadowing, omitted axiom reports and incorrect worker-status handling; the second found unsafe duplicate declarations, theorem-body context substitution and malformed quotient-state restoration. The implementer’s regression tests reproduced conﬁrmed defects and reran the affected cases after repair. Earlier execution tests also exposed cross-project certiﬁcate reuse and unchecked added module parts. Repairs bind inputs, validate original contexts and quotient tags, separate worker events and preserve nonzero-exit dominance. The proposed project-code output-forgery route remains unconﬁrmed. The repaired binary has no independent runtime review.
The frozen checker is e000ed62. Its default shared-checked policy retains a kernel-checked theorem body when a later theorem has the same interface. A crafted serialized proof that passes only by depending on another module’s theorem body can therefore be accepted while ofﬁcial checking rejects it in its declared import context. The accepted proposition is still derived from kernel-checked proofs in the shared checked closure and its reported axioms; this is a different artifact-context guarantee, not original-proof replay parity.
For matching theorem-body context behavior, use -strict-theorem-bodies, which isolates distinct bodies. Both modes explicitly check duplicate bodies skipped by upstream replay; temporary checking names enter neither the shared frontier nor the axiom set. Receipts and checkpoint identities bind the policy and reject incompatible prior state. The strict-mode costs below measure the frozen tool, not the pre-freeze isolation implementation.

Disabling persistence changes Nat.Basic/Grid wall by +3.3/+5.2% and CPU by +12.1/+16.9%. Disabling split fallback changes Nat.Basic/Grid wall by +20.0/+12.4% and CPU by -1.4/-1.4%. For restart, the checker divides replay into segments, saving each completed preﬁx for reuse. Toolchain, artifacts, Lake lock and checkpoint conﬁguration must match; a directory lock protects a live invocation. An interrupted segment remains incomplete. Each receipt separates the accepted preﬁx, current-invocation CPU and previously completed segment CPU, so a cache hit is identiﬁable. It records Lean and dependency versions, module-set hash, accepted and rejected modules, reported axiom sets and CPU/wall clocks. A restart experiment charges restoration and restart overhead.
4.2 Audit coverage and explain trust
Audit connects source inventory, the default import graph and the actual checking receipt. proofserve audit <project> masks comments and strings before counting kernel-decision sites and extracts imports. Supported declarative Lake conﬁguration gives default-root coverage; executable or unsupported conﬁguration yields an explicit unknown unless evaluated roots are supplied. Supplied roots retain their supplied status. The receipt contributes its checked compiled closure and reported axioms; a partial receipt reports the last successful preﬁx. These lists enumerate axiom declarations in accepted modules, rather than minimal per-theorem dependency sets. Per-module costs require explicit source-bound observations.
The tool trusts Lean’s kernel plus its artifact reader, context metadata, checked-token FFI, quiescent marking and editable local checkpoints. Recorded ﬁle identities are noncryptographic; receipt reuse is not remote attestation or a proof of checkpoint integrity. Internal symbols and serialized metadata require a matching SDK. Unknown toolchains fail clearly or explicitly select the matching ofﬁcial checker, identifying that path and its scope. The current receipt lacks a binding to the current source digest; audit exposes this gap. Thus acceptance, source coverage and reported axiom declarations remain separate answers.

5.1 RQ3: original-kernel scaling and alternatives
Nat.Basic/Grid/Barrier check 1,330/3,793/4,449 closure modules; the overlapping counts are not distinct coverage. Table 1 uses three fresh-state repeats per completed point. Barrier 2/4-worker cells were cut to ﬁt the six-hour limit. Eight versus one worker gives 1.70/2.14/1.73× wall speedups for Nat.Basic/Grid/Barrier, with CPU increases of 2.9/11.3/4.6%, respectively. Nat.Basic strict mode costs 4.23× wall and 2.50× CPU versus default at eight workers. Grid strict mode timed out at 1,800 s (at least 5.1× default-eight median); this single censored attempt is not a strict median or full cost.
The 71-cell plan has 61 returned attempts and 10 unattempted cells. Unrun cells are Grid strict r2–3 and Grid/Barrier ofﬁcial default/fresh r2–3 (eight). Nat.Basic ofﬁcial fresh r3 was attempted but stopped at 77.70 s by the total window, not its per-cell cap. No partial observation ﬁlls a completed-run median.
All nine complete ofﬁcial samples accept their requested roots: Nat.Basic default/fresh 3/2, Grid and Barrier 1/1 each. Only Nat.Basic default has a three-repeat median (407.82 s wall). Stock default trusts imports and checks local declarations; fresh replays the ﬁnal imported constant map [4]. 5.2 RQ4: default targets miss a third of decision sites
The full-frame import graph misses 19.78% of source ﬁles and 33.30% of lexical kernel-decision sites at our corpus pin. It covers 130,834 of 163,097 source units (including conﬁguration), leaving 32,263 outside; the omitted ﬁles contain 451,008 of 1,354,379 sites. The earlier report ﬁnds 30,697 ﬁles outside its default graph, about 25% of its 122,459-node source inventory including the root [10]. The revisions and root-count conventions differ, but both demonstrate that a successful default build covers a subset of the source tree. The 33.30% ﬁgure describes decision sites, not module count.
Lexical density makes the workload difference visible even before timing. After masking comments and strings, the AI corpus contains 1,354,379 kernel-decision sites in 5,848 ﬁles; the pinned Mathlib subtree has seven sites in four of 8,529 ﬁles. Normalizing by decimal source megabytes gives a 10,668-fold density ratio. An audit should expose these labels alongside measured costs and uncertainty, rather than convert a site count into CPU work.
Repeated verdicts provide a second, separate kind of evidence. A historical panel checks 2,524 eligible stored-proof targets from three modules at 1, 2, 4 and 8 workers, three repeats each: 30,288 accepted checks and 22,716 one-worker/higher-worker comparisons. Verdicts, diagnostics and allocation counts agree in every observed pair, while elapsed time varies. These ﬁxed-panel observations complement the installed-package rejection controls below.

6 Limitations and related work
Selected cost differences do not deﬁne a universal two-class law. The dependency-ordered compilation preﬁx does not establish representativeness; its missing tail includes memory-intensive families. The original two-draw strata and fragile heavy-tail uncertainty cannot ﬁll that gap. Lexical

5

markers are proxies, not runtime frequencies. Selected closures overlap, module-local replay trusts imports, and target windows exclude shared preparation. Cross-protocol ratios, cache/order and hardware effects limit generalization; historical SubexponentialSAT module-local reference coverage is 14,401/14,721. Failed and blocked modules have no successful cost; their exclusion from cost distributions is explicit. Dynamic phase weights remain unmeasured.
Finite verdict agreement does not prove arbitrary-extension safety, future budget sufﬁciency or soundness of the coordinator. Reported axiom unions retain their actual scope: acceptance can depend on axioms, unsafe/partial exclusions remain visible, and semantic statement faithfulness requires separate review. Current source binding, malicious checkpoint integrity, executable Lake conﬁguration and uncompiled/generated sources require explicit additional evidence. Performance remains bounded to these closures and background conditions; strict Grid and ofﬁcial repeats are incomplete. Final external timings have one observation per arm and ﬁxed order, with common-root rather than full-closure ofﬁcial parity; the AI disclosure is an upstream collection-level attribution, not an independent authorship audit.
Parallel proof checking and asynchronous tasks have precedents in Isabelle, Coq and Lean [13, 1, 3]. Independent checkers and Lake’s shipped workﬂow already address rechecking [7, 6]; the same-corpus tooling report also proposes comparable outcome, timing and export-hash records [10]. Kimina and AXLE supply veriﬁcation services [12, 14]. Our contribution is the measured boundary between checking and recomputation and its integration into a usable original-kernel tool, rather than new parallelism, receipt or serving mechanisms.
7 Conclusion
Matched measurements distinguish cheap proof checking from certiﬁcate recomputation. Observed compilation yields biased-subset P/E lower bounds, not a full-library estimate. ProofServe exposes scope through check, audit and receipts; parallelism saves wall time at higher CPU cost. Default coverage and repeatability establish different facts. The repaired coordinator still needs independent runtime review; external costs retain their unequal checking scopes.
